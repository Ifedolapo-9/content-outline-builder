# Deriving the ICP with the empathy map

Run this every time. The ICP paragraph in the outline is the output of this process, never a guess.

Most outlines describe an audience by job title and stop. That produces content aimed at a category rather than a person in a situation, and it is why so many technical articles open by explaining what the reader already knows.

## Work from evidence, not imagination

Fill the map from what is actually in front of you:

- **The keyword set.** The long tail tells you what the reader is trying to do. Terms like "api", "python", "error" signal a developer mid-task. Terms like "best", "vs", "pricing" signal evaluation. Terms like "what is" with a technical modifier signal someone mid-incident rather than mid-study.
- **The SERP.** What ranks tells you what the searcher accepted as an answer. Note the outliers too, since a thin page ranking well usually means the intent is narrower than the topic.
- **The competitor pages.** Their assumed reader is visible in their opening paragraph.
- **The house style guide**, if the client has one. It usually names the audience explicitly.
- **The product's real use cases**, where the client is a vendor.

## The four quadrants

### 1. What do they SEE

What surrounds them daily. Tools, trends, content they consume. What influences their choices. What competitors or alternatives they are comparing. What documentation or blogs they read.

For technical audiences this is usually: their own terminal or logs, their stack, the specific error in front of them, Stack Overflow and GitHub issues before anything else, then forums, then vendor blogs which they read while discounting.

Name the alternatives they are weighing. That set is often wider than the article assumes, and articles fail by comparing within one category when the reader is choosing between categories.

### 2. What do they THINK and FEEL

What matters to them logically and emotionally. What they value: simplicity, speed, depth. What frustrates them about tools, docs and workflows. What they are worried about.

Split these two deliberately. The logical column is what they will tell a colleague; the emotional column is why they are reading at 11pm. Both belong in the map, and the second one usually determines whether they keep reading.

### 3. What do they SAY and DO

The language they use, including the imprecise version. The actions they take. The questions they ask in forums. The keywords and phrases they repeat. What they share, comment on, or ignore.

Capture their vocabulary verbatim where you can, because it belongs in the article. A reader searching "getting blocked" should not land on a page that only says "access denial mitigation."

Also record how they read. Skimmers who jump to the first code block need a different structure from people reading start to finish.

### 4. What are their PAINS and GAINS

**Pains.** What is stopping them. What makes the product or task hard. What confuses or overwhelms them. Include the pain they would not admit to, such as not knowing which of five overlapping product categories they actually need.

**Gains.** What a win looks like after reading. The outcome they want. How the content makes their job easier. Push past "they learn about X" to what they can now do, decide, or defend to someone else.

## The summary paragraph

Two to three sentences on tone, needs, technical level and learning style. This is what goes into the outline's ICP block.

Good: "Working backend and data engineers whose scraper broke this week, arriving mid-incident with a purchase half-decided and a strong prior that every article they open is an advert. Technically fluent but not anti-bot specialists, they skim headings and tables before reading prose, and they trust sources that concede a weakness more than sources that sound confident."

Weak: "Developers interested in web scraping who want to learn more about available tools."

The difference is that the first one tells a writer what to do.

## What the map changes in the outline

Once done, check the outline against it:

- **Does the opening meet them where they are?** A reader mid-incident does not need history.
- **Does the structure match how they read?** Skimmers need the answer in the first paragraph of each section.
- **Is their vocabulary used?** Including the imprecise version, at least once, so search matches.
- **Is a stated pain answered by a named section?** If a major pain has no home in the outline, add one.
- **Are you writing for the wrong reader?** Note explicitly who the piece is not for, especially where a large adjacent audience ranks for the same term. Chasing them dilutes the piece.

## Deliverable

Include the summary paragraph plus the situation and skill-level bullets in the outline itself. Offer the full four-quadrant map as a separate document if the user wants it, or if the audience is unfamiliar enough that the reasoning is worth showing.
