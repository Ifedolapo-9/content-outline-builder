# Writing the outline

How to fill the slots so a writer can act on them.

## Competitor gap analysis

Two to three competitors. More is padding.

For each, name **one strength worth matching** and **one gap to exploit**. Both must be specific. "Well written" is not a strength; "the only page on the SERP that explains token mechanics" is.

The gap is stronger when you can say why it exists structurally:

- **Position.** A solver vendor cannot recommend not solving. A proxy company cannot tell you to reduce request volume. Their business model forbids the honest version, and that is a gap no amount of effort closes.
- **Format.** A glossary page cannot go deep. A listicle cannot carry a methodology.
- **Authority.** A generalist site can write well about a technical topic but cannot report first-hand testing.
- **Age.** A page that ranks on domain strength while being three years stale.

Follow the table with a short paragraph naming the two or three openings the outline actually takes. Two well-chosen gaps beat six observations.

**Where to look for gaps that matter:** what every competitor covers the same way, what none of them covers, what one of them does well that the others ignore, and what the People Also Ask box asks that no page answers.

## Action labels, for refreshes

Every existing section gets one:

- **Keep.** Accurate and working. Leave it.
- **Keep and tighten.** Right content, too long or unfocused.
- **Rework.** Right topic, wrong execution. Say what the new version does differently.
- **New.** Does not exist yet.
- **Remove.** Say where its useful content goes, and flag any internal links living inside it, because those need rehoming.

Labels without direction are useless. "Rework" tells a writer nothing; "Rework: the current answer is a flat yes, which is wrong and undermines the rest of the page. New answer routes by situation" tells them everything.

## Directional guidance

The slot that separates a usable outline from a table of contents. Write what the writer should **do**.

| Weak | Strong |
| :---- | :---- |
| "Discuss the types of X" | "Lead with a comparison block covering the four types across five dimensions, then one short subsection each. The honest-limitation column does the work; a list where every option sounds good is a list nobody trusts." |
| "Explain pricing" | "Open with the arithmetic rather than an adjective. Source every figure from the vendor's own page on the day of publication and date-stamp it in the copy." |
| "Cover the limitations" | "Write as plain assessment rather than hedging. Competing pages are increasingly explicit that a tool working in a demo is not a tool working in production, and matching that candour is what earns the citation." |

Useful verbs: open with, lead with, close on, absorb from, route by, name explicitly, state plainly, cut, fold into.

Where a specific opening will be quoted or extracted, specify its shape and length: "a 40 to 80 word definition written to stand alone as a quotable answer."

## Rationale

Include it only where the decision is contestable: a section being cut, a reordering, a placement that looks wrong, a concession that costs something.

One or two sentences. Cite evidence where it exists, whether SERP behaviour, ranking data, or a documented case study.

Mark rationale as removable. Once decisions are settled it becomes noise, and clients often ask for it to be stripped before the outline goes to a writer.

## Internal links

Place each link **in the section where the reader needs it**. A link in the section that stops short of the detail gets clicked. The same link in a footer list does not.

Descriptive anchor text, always. "How to rotate proxies in Python", never "read more" or "this article".

Verify every link before it goes in the outline. A 404 in a brief wastes the writer's time and is trivially avoidable.

For a refresh, check which links currently live in sections marked Remove and rehome them explicitly.

## Word budgets

Optional, and useful mainly for keeping proportion: it stops a definitional section running longer than the comparison a reader came for. Omit if the user does not want them.

If a section budget exceeds about 600 words, it probably wants splitting.

## FAQ planning

Six to eight questions. Each gets a one-line steer, not just the question text.

The FAQ is where commercial long-tail terms live that would look stuffed in body prose. Assign one term per question and say so.

Note the schema expectation honestly: FAQPage markup is worth applying, but rich results have been restricted to government and health sites since 2023, so the return is People Also Ask coverage and citation in AI answers.

Answers should be self-contained, 40 to 60 words, and written so they make sense lifted out of the page.

## Editorial dependencies

The section that saves a review round. Surface anything the writer will hit and cannot resolve alone:

- Internal links that 404 or redirect
- Figures needing verification and a date stamp
- Contradictions between existing copy, tables and FAQs
- Promo codes, offers or pricing that may have expired
- Assets required: diagrams, screenshots, test data
- Anything needing a client decision, such as product positioning or a legal claim

Finding these at outline stage is the whole point. Finding them in revision costs a round.

## Common failures

**A table of contents wearing an outline's clothes.** Headings with no direction under them. Test: could the writer act on this without asking a question?

**An angle that is not a claim.** "We will cover X comprehensively" is a promise, not an angle. If nobody could disagree with it, it is not doing any work.

**Competitor analysis as summary.** Describing what competitors contain rather than what they miss. The output of the analysis is the openings you take, not an inventory.

**Links dumped at the end.** They end up in a footer nobody reads.

**A methodology promised but not specified.** For any piece claiming testing, specify targets, criteria, exclusions and run count at outline stage. This is the single most common cause of a comparison article failing review.

**Unstated assumptions.** If you assumed the word count, the audience, or that a competitor is the real one to beat, say so. The writer needs to know which parts are settled and which are yours.

**Rationale everywhere.** Justifying every heading buries the two or three decisions that actually need defending.
